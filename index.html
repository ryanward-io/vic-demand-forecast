Victorian electricity demand forecast

Every night, nine models forecast Victoria's electricity demand for the next 48 hours, from rules of thumb to AI that has never seen this data. All are scored against what happened, and against AEMO's own forecast.

    Why forecast demand?

    Power has to be generated the moment it's used, so the grid needs to know a day or two ahead how much Victoria will want. Guess low and it scrambles for expensive power; guess high and it pays for generators it didn't need. Heat, cold snaps and rooftop solar all move the number.

    What's being tested

    Whether new AI forecasting models, trained on huge numbers of other series but never this one, can match models built for the job, and how close any of them get to AEMO, which runs the grid.

    Short version

    AEMO is still best, off by about 4% on average. The best of mine is off by about 5%. Chronos-2, an AI model that has never seen this data, ties with a model trained on seven years of it.

    How each model did over the past year

    A backtest replays the past. On every day from 1 Oct 2025 to 28 Sep 2026, each model forecast the next two days using only what was known at midnight, and was then checked against what happened.

    Scroll the table sideways for more →

    Average miss is how far the forecast was from actual demand, in megawatts (MW) and as a percentage. "vs last week" compares each model with the lazy guess that this week repeats last week: 50 means it missed by half as much. Lower is better.

    What it shows

    • AEMO's own forecast is still the one to beat: off by 4.0% today and 4.4% tomorrow, against 4.9% and 5.6% for the best of mine, which works from one Melbourne weather point and public data.
    • Weather and calendar matter most. LightGBM and the average roughly halve the benchmarks' error; the LSTM cuts it by about a third.
    • Chronos-2, with no training on this data, ties with LightGBM trained on seven years of it.
    • Their average is the best of mine: its average miss is about 19 MW smaller than LightGBM's and 30 MW smaller than Chronos-2's, and it's closer on about 70% of days. I picked the pairing before running the backtest, not for scoring well.
    • Timestral, Mistral's newer AI model, also ties with both. Its mistakes look a lot like Chronos-2's, so swapping it into the average gains only about 4 MW. Its likely range is better (it held the actual demand 78% of the time, against Chronos-2's 74%), but it's also wider.
    • The LSTM beats the benchmarks but trails the trees, most of all in winter, which is what I'd expect: on one series of this size, boosted trees usually win.
    How the backtest works

    Each model stands at midnight and forecasts the next 48 half-hours. After midnight it sees the weather forecast that existed at the time (archived by Open-Meteo), never the weather that actually happened. Models that learn are retrained at the start of each month on data up to that day (live, they retrain weekly). Two models "tie" when a 95% bootstrap interval for the difference, resampling whole days, includes zero.

    AEMO isn't quite like for like. In the backtest my models used weather forecasts made a day or two earlier, while AEMO used its latest. Its forecast also joins two reports (pre-dispatch for the first 28 hours, then ST PASA), which may not define demand identically: its average error shifts by about 70 MW where they meet.

    The models

    From simplest to most complex. The simple ones look only at past demand; the rest also get the weather forecast and the calendar.

    Same as yesterday · Same day last week · Average of last 4 weeks
    Benchmarks. Anything cleverer has to beat these to be worth running.
    MSTL + ETS
    Classical statistics: split the last 8 weeks into a trend plus daily and weekly cycles, then extend them forward. Demand only, no weather.
    LightGBM (calendar + weather)
    Gradient-boosted decision trees, the workhorse of forecasting competitions: hundreds of small if-then rules, learned from 2019 onwards, for how demand shifts with temperature, sunshine, day of week and public holidays.
    LSTM
    A small neural network trained from scratch: one part reads the last week, another walks through the next two days using the weather forecast and calendar.
    Chronos-2 (AI foundation model)
    Amazon's pretrained time-series model (2025). It has never been trained on this data: it reads the last 12 weeks of demand with weather and holidays and forecasts "zero-shot", the way a chatbot answers a question it was never specifically taught.
    Timestral (AI foundation model)
    Mistral's pretrained time-series model (October 2026, the 209-million-parameter version), given exactly the same inputs as Chronos-2. Mistral reports it tops the public forecasting benchmarks; this is an independent test on one series. It came out after the backtest year, so it may have seen this data in training; its live record is the cleaner test.
    Average of LightGBM and Chronos-2
    A simple ensemble: the two make different kinds of mistakes, so their average is often better than either.
    AEMO's own forecast (the reference)
    What the market operator itself expected: its pre-dispatch forecast from about 23:30 for the next 28 hours, then the middle estimate (the one demand is equally likely to land above or below) from its week-ahead ST PASA forecast of about 22:00. AEMO's forecasters have far more to work with (many weather stations, rooftop solar and large-load models, and constant updates), so this is the bar to aim at, not a fair fight.
