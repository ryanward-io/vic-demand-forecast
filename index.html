    Backtest: how each model would have done

    Every day in the window, each model stands at midnight with only the data known by then and forecasts the next 48 hours. After midnight it sees the weather forecast that existed at the time (archived by Open-Meteo), never the weather that actually happened, and models that learn are retrained at the start of each month on data up to that day (live, they retrain weekly). MAE is the average miss in megawatts; MAPE the same as a percentage. "vs last week" compares each model with the lazy guess that this week repeats last week: for every 100 MW that guess missed by over the two days, how many the model missed by. Lower is better.

    What the backtest shows (Oct 2025 – Sep 2026)

    • Weather and calendar matter most: every model that uses them roughly halves the benchmarks' error.
    • Chronos-2, with no training on this data, ties with LightGBM trained on seven years of it (the difference is within noise).
    • Their average is the best forecast, by about 30 MW a day over either alone (95% bootstrap interval 23–43 MW), and better on about 70% of days. The ensemble was chosen before the backtest was run, not picked for scoring well.
    • Timestral, Mistral's newer foundation model, also ties: 3 MW a day better than Chronos-2 (95% interval 13 better to 6 worse) and 7 MW worse than LightGBM (5 better to 18 worse). Its errors track Chronos-2's closely, so swapping it into the average gains only about 4 MW a day, also within noise. Where it is ahead is its range, which held the actual demand about 78% of the time against Chronos-2's 74%.
    • The LSTM beats the benchmarks but trails the trees, most of all in winter, which matches the literature: on one series of this size, boosted trees usually win.

    The models

    Same as yesterday · Same day last week · Average of last 4 weeks
    Benchmarks. Anything cleverer has to beat these to be worth running.
    MSTL + ETS
    Classical statistics: split the last 8 weeks into a trend plus daily and weekly cycles, then extend the trend with exponential smoothing. Demand only, no weather.
    LightGBM (calendar + weather)
    Gradient-boosted decision trees, the workhorse of forecasting competitions. Learns from 2019 onwards how demand on a given half-hour departs from its recent average, given the weather forecast, solar radiation, day of week and public holidays.
    LSTM (seq2seq)
    A small recurrent neural network trained from scratch: one LSTM reads the last week, a second walks through the next two days using the weather forecast and calendar.
    Chronos-2 (foundation model)
    Amazon's pretrained time-series transformer (2025). It has never been trained on this data: it reads the last 12 weeks of demand with weather and holidays and forecasts "zero-shot", the way a language model answers without fine-tuning.
    Timestral (foundation model)
    Mistral's pretrained time-series model (October 2026, the 209-million-parameter version), also zero-shot and given exactly the same inputs as Chronos-2, so the two compare like for like. Mistral reports it tops the public forecasting benchmarks; this is an independent test on one series.
    AEMO's own forecast (the reference)
    What the market operator itself expected: its pre-dispatch run issued at about 23:30 for the next 28 hours, then the 50% probability-of-exceedance demand from its last ST PASA run before midnight. AEMO's forecasters have far more to work with (many weather stations, rooftop solar and large-load models, and constant updates), so this is the bar to aim at, not a fair fight.
    Average of LightGBM and Chronos-2
    A simple ensemble: the two make different kinds of mistakes, so their average is often better than either.
